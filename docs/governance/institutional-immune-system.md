# Institutional Immune System — BASELINE

**Decision basis:** Decision 0007  
**Institutional basis:** `VISION.md`, `GOVERNANCE.md`, `ROADMAP.md`, and Decision 0003  
**Operational loop:** `docs/governance/feedback-to-governed-work.md`

## Purpose

The Institutional Immune System is Calypso’s Promise’s cross-cutting corrigibility layer.

It exists so the project can discover when a premise, metric, incentive, implementation, policy, authority structure, or interpretation is wrong; limit the resulting harm; reverse or replace the responsible mechanism; restore affected people and state where possible; and preserve the evidence needed to avoid repeating the failure.

The governing principle is:

> Every mechanism powerful enough to improve the system must also expose a proportionate way to prove it wrong, constrain its harm, reverse or replace it, and preserve what was learned.

The immune system is not a department that owns every correction. It is a shared architecture of records, interfaces, duties, tests, challenge paths, separation of powers, and recovery behavior used by every consequential module and institution.

## Metaphor boundary

The immune-system metaphor applies to the project’s ability to detect and correct harmful **conditions and mechanisms**. It must never be used to describe critics, users, contributors, minorities, dissenters, competitors, or disfavored groups as pathogens, infections, foreign bodies, or threats merely because they disagree.

A person who demonstrates that the system is wrong is performing a legitimate immune function. The target of correction is the unsafe condition, failed premise, captured process, harmful behavior, or defective mechanism—not the person raising it.

Conduct, security, abuse, and safety cases still require evidence, due process, proportional response, and the applicable private-information boundaries.

## Relationship to the Promise

Calypso’s Promise separates three levels of change.

### The Promise

The Promise contains the project’s protected purpose and rights floor, including personal sovereignty, meaningful consent and refusal, private-by-default operation, personal value before secondary use, non-punitive return, public benefit, transparency, portability, and progressive founder independence.

These commitments are difficult to change. They are not ordinary empirical hypotheses that may be discarded merely because a short-term metric improves after weakening them.

The immune system protects the Promise while still permitting constitutional challenge through the elevated decision process when evidence shows contradiction, incompleteness, or serious unintended harm.

### Doctrine

Doctrine contains the current best methods for fulfilling the Promise: incentive policies, governance phases, risk thresholds, review models, health interpretations, prioritization dimensions, operating rules, and institutional strategies.

Doctrine must be:

- versioned
- evidence-backed
- explicit about uncertainty
- challengeable
- reversible where possible
- periodically revalidated
- replaceable through legitimate authority

### Implementation

Implementation includes interfaces, models, algorithms, services, providers, frameworks, schemas, formulas, game mechanics, workflows, and infrastructure.

Implementation should be aggressively replaceable within the frozen trust and rights boundaries. Technical familiarity, sunk cost, vendor dependence, contributor prestige, or historical authorship does not make an implementation permanent.

The change gradient is therefore:

> rigid about rights and purpose; cautious and evidence-led about doctrine; modular and replaceable in implementation.

## Scope

The immune system applies across the following planes.

### Epistemic

- assumptions and causal claims
- observations, evidence, inference, confidence, and uncertainty
- metrics and proxies
- AI-generated interpretations
- health and scientific claims
- disagreement, contradiction, supersession, and unknown states

### Technical and data

- architecture and service boundaries
- source, normalization, provenance, and derived records
- migrations, deletion, export, restoration, and rollback
- models, validators, infrastructure, providers, and dependencies
- security, privacy, reliability, accessibility, and data-integrity failures

### Product and behavioral

- user outcomes and unintended harms
- game mechanics and progression
- engagement, return, anxiety, compulsion, confusion, and coercion
- incentives and strategic behavior
- consent comprehension and refusal behavior
- distributional effects across populations and access needs

### Governance and institutional

- authority, delegation, representation, concentration, capture, and removal
- priority policies, overrides, appeals, and outcome review
- founder, maintainer, specialist, contributor, player, donor, and council power
- public claims, conflicts, spending, partnerships, and institutional legitimacy
- emergency powers, succession, exit, fork, merger, and dissolution

### Economic and research

- business models, fees, compensation, grants, treasury, and public-good allocation
- incentives for disclosure, participation, recruitment, contribution, and research
- conflicts, concentration, adverse selection, extraction, and speculation
- study assumptions, participant protections, access, withdrawal, results, and benefit

## Core modules

The modules below are logical responsibilities. Early phases may implement them through documents, issues, reviews, tests, and named duties rather than production services.

### 1. Outcome and Guardrail Observatory

Records what a mechanism intends to improve and what it must not degrade.

Every material mechanism should declare, proportionate to consequence:

- intended outcome
- target or affected population
- leading and lagging indicators
- guardrail metrics
- possible harms and failure modes
- baseline and comparison method
- observation period
- missing-data behavior
- known confounders
- disaggregations needed to reveal concentrated harm
- responsible evaluator
- review and expiration date

A mechanism may not define success only through a metric it directly controls. Delivery, usage, engagement, issue closure, revenue, participation, or a model score is not automatically equivalent to human benefit.

### 2. Assumption Registry

Maintains material beliefs that connect actions to expected outcomes.

The registry includes causal, behavioral, technical, governance, economic, and operational assumptions. Each record preserves evidence for and against the claim, confidence, dependencies, affected groups, review timing, and invalidation conditions.

The canonical registry is `docs/governance/assumption-registry.md`.

### 3. Challenge and Dissent Protocol

Provides a structured path to dispute:

- an assumption
- a metric or proxy
- an interpretation or evidence claim
- an incentive or progression rule
- an architecture or implementation
- a governance or priority decision
- an authority transfer
- a public claim or institutional behavior
- a safety or rights boundary
- the immune system itself

A challenge may show a defect without providing a complete replacement. The burden of producing a full solution must not silence evidence of harm.

Challenges are assessed by evidence, affectedness, risk, rights implications, uncertainty, reproducibility, and falsifiability—not by the challenger’s popularity, status, wealth, contribution volume, or agreement with current leadership.

Public challenges use only PUBLIC or synthetic information. Protected health, account, security, conduct, legal, correspondence, personnel, or third-party evidence requires the applicable private route and may produce only a reviewed, minimized public derivative.

### 4. Containment and Emergency Control

Limits active harm while ordinary analysis and governance continue.

Containment may include:

- pausing a release, model, policy, integration, study, campaign, reward, authority, or data flow
- disabling or rate-limiting a feature
- revoking credentials or access
- isolating a provider or dependency
- suspending an automated decision
- preserving evidence
- narrowing a population or rollout
- switching to a safer fallback
- blocking further propagation or publication

Containment is not a verdict. It must not be treated as proof of guilt, permanent policy, constitutional amendment, or authorization to redesign unrelated systems.

Emergency action must be narrow, logged, attributable, time-limited, independently reviewable, and followed by restoration and a safe public record when possible.

### 5. Experiment, Reversibility, and Rollout Control

Moves consequential changes through a staged path:

1. proposal
2. simulation or tabletop review
3. synthetic or isolated validation
4. limited and consent-appropriate trial
5. monitored rollout
6. broader adoption
7. scheduled revalidation
8. sunset, replacement, or renewal

Supporting controls may include:

- feature flags
- policy revisions
- canary releases
- bounded cohorts
- reversible migrations
- immutable source versions
- data snapshots where lawful and safe
- automatic rollback thresholds
- sunset clauses
- fallback interfaces
- vendor and model replacement paths
- documented recovery procedures

Every material proposal should answer:

- What can be rolled back?
- What cannot be rolled back?
- How quickly can authority or behavior be contained?
- What state, evidence, or user trust may remain damaged?
- How will affected people be notified and restored?
- What would justify continuing despite residual risk?

### 6. Appeal, Adjudication, and Restoration

Provides a path for affected people to contest consequential outcomes and obtain correction.

Depending on the decision class, rights may include:

- explanation
- access to the relevant evidence and provenance
- correction or amendment
- independent review
- recusal and conflict disclosure
- temporary suspension of an adverse consequence
- export, withdrawal, or meaningful exit
- restoration of access, records, standing, contribution credit, rewards, or other state
- notification of downstream recipients where correction has propagated
- an explicit residual-harm record when full restoration is impossible

The project does not yet establish a permanent tribunal, ombuds office, or judicial body. Current appeals follow existing maintainer, specialist, incident, and decision-record authority while later phases establish independent functions through published charters.

### 7. Audit and Revalidation

Tests whether accepted mechanisms remain useful, legitimate, safe, comprehensible, and aligned.

Revalidation may be:

- scheduled
- triggered by evidence or incident
- required at a phase gate
- required before authority expansion
- required after a material override
- required after a failed outcome
- required after a migration, vendor change, or leadership transition
- requested through a structured challenge

Some assumptions and policies should expire unless affirmatively renewed. The burden of continued justification should increase with consequence, age, concentration of power, uncertainty, and irreversibility.

Review must seek evidence that would disconfirm the current view, not merely evidence that supports it.

### 8. Voice, Exit, Portability, and Fork

Preserves final defenses against institutional capture.

Participants need:

- **Voice:** a meaningful internal path to report, challenge, appeal, propose, and participate.
- **Exit:** the ability to leave without punitive lock-in and retain the rights, exports, and records promised by the applicable domain.
- **Portability:** usable data, schemas, provenance, and interfaces that do not make exit nominal.
- **Fork:** a credible ability to continue public infrastructure and mission under alternative stewardship when legitimate internal correction fails.

Forkability requires more than a public license. It depends on reproducible builds, open specifications, portable records, transparent decisions, separable modules, documented operations, and non-proprietary dependencies where practical.

## Correction lifecycle

Every consequential loop should be able to connect to the following lifecycle.

### 1. Observe

Collect intended outcomes, guardrails, user reports, incident evidence, audits, appeals, anomalies, contradictions, external evidence, and distributional effects.

### 2. Detect

Identify a potential mismatch between observed behavior and:

- intended outcome
- protected right
- declared assumption
- safety or quality threshold
- published policy
- expected distribution of benefit and harm
- institutional mandate

### 3. Attribute

Identify the mechanism, dependency, assumption, authority, incentive, implementation, or external condition that may explain the mismatch. Attribution may remain disputed or probabilistic.

### 4. Challenge

Create a challenge record that states the disputed claim, evidence, affected groups, urgency, uncertainty, falsification method, and possible containment or alternative.

### 5. Contain

When delay could compound harm, apply the narrowest effective temporary control. Preserve evidence and due process.

### 6. Test

Use the least harmful method capable of distinguishing competing explanations: reproduction, review, simulation, synthetic fixture, audit, limited experiment, comparison, specialist analysis, or participant validation.

### 7. Decide

Retain, modify, narrow, replace, pause, sunset, reverse, or escalate the mechanism through the authority appropriate to the decision class.

### 8. Restore

Correct affected records and state, remove or reduce ongoing consequences, notify affected people and downstream systems where appropriate, and document what cannot be restored.

### 9. Learn

Update the Assumption Registry, decision record, tests, documentation, issue history, policy revision, training, and governance precedent.

### 10. Revalidate

Schedule the next review rather than treating the corrected mechanism as permanently settled.

The feedback-to-governed-work loop handles ordinary product and institutional improvement. The immune system surrounds it and asks whether the loop’s evidence, priority definitions, incentives, authority, and claimed outcomes remain worthy of being followed.

## Record types

Future typed contracts should preserve distinct records for:

- **Assumption** — a material belief connecting an action, condition, or mechanism to an expected result.
- **Observation** — evidence without an authoritative interpretation embedded in the observation itself.
- **Challenge** — a structured dispute or revalidation request.
- **Containment** — a temporary action limiting scope, authority, or propagation.
- **Test or review** — a method and result used to distinguish competing claims.
- **Decision** — accountable resolution with authority, rationale, conflicts, and review timing.
- **Override** — departure from a policy, score, default, or prior decision.
- **Restoration** — repair of affected people, records, access, state, or institutional standing.
- **Residual harm** — a consequence that remains after reasonable correction.
- **Learning update** — changes to assumptions, policy, tests, docs, or governance precedent.
- **Revalidation** — later review of whether the mechanism remains justified.

These records must preserve provenance and may not be collapsed into one mutable status field that erases history.

## Uncertainty model

Material claims should distinguish:

- observed
- user-reported
- source-reported
- calculated
- inferred
- interpreted
- disputed
- provisional
- independently reproduced
- specialist-reviewed
- accepted policy
- superseded
- invalidated
- unknown

Confidence may inform behavior but does not itself create authority. A highly confident model output cannot override player confirmation, specialist duties, constitutional rights, or accountable governance boundaries.

Lower confidence or higher consequence may require:

- softer or more explicit language
- additional evidence
- independent review
- a narrower rollout
- reduced automation
- a stronger fallback
- no action until uncertainty is reduced

## Proportional scrutiny

Friction must scale with consequence.

### Routine

Examples: copy edits, low-risk documentation, internal refactors with no trust-boundary change.

Expected controls: ordinary review, tests where relevant, easy correction.

### Material

Examples: player-facing behavior, incentive changes, content rules, significant architecture, analytics definitions, roadmap policies.

Expected controls: declared outcomes and assumptions, guardrails, versioning, review, rollback, outcome check.

### High consequence

Examples: health interpretations, consent, privacy, security, accessibility, canonical records, research, money, eligibility, identity, governance authority.

Expected controls: specialist and affected-party review, independent challenge, staged rollout, strong provenance, appeal, restoration, explicit residual risk.

### Constitutional or systemically irreversible

Examples: rights, mission, founder powers, dissolution, public release of protected material, irreversible asset transfer, broad governance or economic mechanisms.

Expected controls: decision record, elevated authority, independent adversarial review, public rationale, explicit minority protection, irreversible-effects analysis, renewal or sunset, and a credible continuity or fork path.

The exact labels and thresholds remain unresolved until tested. The principle is that a typography change must not require constitutional process, while a consent or authority change must not pass as ordinary implementation.

## Independence and separation of powers

Independence is relative to the decision class and institutional phase.

The project should progressively separate:

- proposal from approval
- implementation from validation
- metric ownership from outcome review
- executive authority from audit
- incident containment from permanent policy
- specialist advice from unrelated governance control
- funding from constitutional authority
- challenge triage from final appeal

During Phase 0, full independence is not always possible. The limitation must be explicit. Material self-review by the founding steward or sole maintainer should be recorded as a key-person and independence risk rather than represented as independent validation.

No constituency should permanently control enough of proposal, execution, monitoring, audit, appeal, emergency action, and constitutional change to make itself effectively unreviewable.

## Challenge protocol

A public-safe challenge should identify:

1. target record, mechanism, claim, or decision
2. the disputed premise or observed failure
3. public or synthetic evidence
4. affected people, rights, or systems
5. severity, urgency, and possible compounding harm
6. uncertainty and competing explanations
7. a falsification or review method
8. requested containment, when any
9. proposed alternative or acceptance conditions, when known
10. conflicts, relationships, or relevant expertise
11. information-classification confirmation

A challenge may result in:

- request for safe evidence
- duplicate or related-record linkage
- ordinary work item
- scheduled revalidation
- specialist review
- independent review
- limited test
- containment
- policy or implementation change
- restoration
- rejection with rationale
- closure as unsupported, bad faith, unsafe for public handling, or outside scope
- reopening when later evidence changes the result

Closure is not erasure. The original claim, evidence, decision, and later correction remain part of institutional memory.

## Bad faith, abuse, and paralysis controls

The project must protect challenge while resisting abuse.

Potential abuse includes:

- spam or duplicate flooding
- harassment disguised as review
- public pressure to disclose protected evidence
- strategic delay
- repetitive claims with no new evidence
- brigading or synthetic consensus
- donor, contributor, expert, or maintainer intimidation
- emergency claims used to bypass ordinary authority
- challenges created to manufacture contribution credit

Controls may include safe triage, duplicate linking, evidence requests, rate limits, moderation, conflict disclosure, scoped review, time-boxing, appeal, random audit, and private routing.

Bad faith must not be inferred merely from disagreement, low status, unfamiliar communication style, a minority position, or criticism of leadership. Restrictions and moderation decisions must remain explainable and appealable.

## Incentive integration

Immune-system contributions may include:

- disproving a report or assumption
- reproducing a failure
- identifying a misleading metric
- detecting a minority or accessibility harm
- finding a perverse incentive
- providing adversarial review
- improving a falsification test
- designing a reversible rollout
- validating containment or restoration
- correcting an authority or priority decision
- performing a revalidation review
- documenting a failed experiment or negative result

The project must not reward:

- challenge volume by itself
- controversy or outrage
- unnecessary disclosure
- manufactured incidents
- repeated low-value objections
- obstruction without evidence or rights basis
- creating a defect and then claiming correction credit

Credit remains scoped, reviewable, correctable, non-transferable by default, and unable to create universal governance power.

## Metrics

The immune system should measure its own health without optimizing for raw challenge volume.

Useful measures may include:

- percentage of material mechanisms with declared assumptions, outcomes, guardrails, and review timing
- time from credible detection to safe containment
- percentage of high-consequence changes with a tested rollback or fallback
- challenge resolution and appeal outcomes by decision class
- independent reproduction rate
- correction, reopen, rollback, and restoration rates
- residual-harm records and time to follow-up
- stale assumptions and overdue revalidations
- concentration of proposal, approval, audit, appeal, and emergency authority
- minority, accessibility, and low-frequency harms surfaced
- emergency activations, duration, renewal, scope expansion, and post-incident review
- percentage of failed or neutral outcomes published rather than hidden
- participant comprehension of consequential decisions and appeal paths
- recurring failure patterns converted into tests or policy changes

Challenge count, closure count, average confidence, and speed alone are not sufficient success measures.

## Minimum viable validation

Before automation, the repository should validate the cheapest objective boundaries.

### Record completeness

- material assumptions have stable identifiers and review dates
- challenges name a target and disputed claim
- containment names scope, authority, reason, and expiration
- decisions preserve evidence, conflicts, rationale, appeal, and review timing
- restoration distinguishes repaired state from unresolved residual harm
- supersession and invalidation preserve history

### Rights and information safety

- public challenge intake prohibits protected source material
- no challenge mechanism requires broader health-data consent or public identity disclosure
- majority signals cannot bypass protected rights or specialist duties
- people are not categorized as threats through the immune-system metaphor
- private evidence can produce a safe reviewed derivative without exposing its source

### Governance and independence

- the decision class and current authority are explicit
- self-review and independence limitations are disclosed
- emergency actions automatically expire
- containment cannot silently become permanent policy
- appeal and review paths are present for consequential decisions
- no score or AI output silently creates authority

### Reversibility and outcome

- material changes state rollback and irreversible effects
- synthetic failures can trigger expected containment
- release and delivery are distinguished from outcome
- failed outcomes can reopen or reverse a mechanism
- restoration and residual harm are recorded separately
- revalidation can supersede a prior accepted conclusion

### Synthetic fixtures

Future tests should include:

- a popular metric that rises while user wellbeing declines
- a high-engagement game mechanic that creates compulsive or coercive behavior
- an incentive that rewards excess disclosure despite an explicit prohibition
- a low-frequency accessibility harm hidden by aggregate success
- a confident AI interpretation contradicted by source evidence
- a priority policy that repeatedly requires maintainer override
- an assumption retained after its evidence expires
- a challenge filed by a low-status participant with stronger evidence than leadership
- a bad-faith challenge campaign that must be limited without silencing legitimate dissent
- an emergency brake activated correctly, one activated too broadly, and one renewed improperly
- a technically successful rollback that fails to restore affected users
- a review body captured by the same constituency it audits
- a fork path that is nominally open but operationally unusable

## Implementation sequence

### Step 0 — Repository baseline

- accept Decision 0007
- publish this architecture
- seed the Assumption Registry
- add a public-safe challenge issue form
- link the category from the repository README

### Step 1 — Manual operating protocol

- define record templates and status vocabularies
- map existing assumptions, guardrails, emergency powers, appeals, and rollback paths
- assign current Phase 0 responsibilities and disclose independence limitations
- select a small set of material assumptions for manual revalidation
- connect challenge outcomes to ordinary governed work

### Step 2 — Contracts and validation

- define typed records for assumptions, challenges, containment, review, restoration, and revalidation
- add deterministic validators and synthetic fixtures
- require proportionate immune-system fields in material proposals and decision records
- publish overdue review and concentration reports

### Step 3 — Product and operational integration

- provide accessible in-product challenge and appeal paths with safe private routing
- connect outcome and guardrail observability to releases, incentives, models, and governance pilots
- implement staged rollout, containment, rollback, and restoration controls
- expose understandable decision, uncertainty, and appeal records

### Step 4 — Independent and distributed functions

- charter bounded independent review, ombuds, audit, or constitutional functions only after phase gates
- distribute emergency and recovery authority
- test leadership transition, vendor exit, governance rollback, and fork readiness
- publish institutional immune-system performance and failures

## Explicitly unresolved

The following require later evidence and decisions:

- exact record schemas and identifiers
- confidence and scrutiny vocabularies
- review cadences by decision class
- challenge triage ownership and service levels
- private challenge and protected-disclosure systems
- independent reviewer selection and compensation
- ombuds, tribunal, appellate, or constitutional-review structure
- emergency multi-party authorization and break-glass design
- restoration duties, remedies, and compensation
- public dashboards and privacy-preserving metrics
- challenge contribution credit, decay, dispute, and anti-gaming rules
- audit sampling and external-review requirements
- technical rollback standards by domain
- fork, migration, merger, dissolution, and mission-asset procedures

These mechanisms must remain unresolved until they can be tested without weakening the Promise, exposing protected information, centralizing unreviewable authority, or creating procedural paralysis.

## Success condition

The Institutional Immune System is working when:

- important assumptions are visible before they become invisible infrastructure
- evidence against a favored premise can travel farther than the status of its author
- consequential mechanisms expose outcomes, guardrails, challenge, appeal, rollback, restoration, and revalidation
- uncertainty changes behavior rather than appearing only as a disclaimer
- emergency action contains harm without becoming permanent shadow governance
- minority and user rights survive popularity, capital, expertise, urgency, and executive pressure
- failed outcomes are published and converted into better tests and policies
- authority to correct the system is itself correctable
- participants retain meaningful voice, exit, portability, and a credible fork path
- each correction cycle makes both the product and the institution more capable of discovering their next mistake

The project is not healthy because it never fails. It is healthy when failure can be detected early, challenged safely, contained proportionately, corrected openly, and transformed into durable institutional learning.
