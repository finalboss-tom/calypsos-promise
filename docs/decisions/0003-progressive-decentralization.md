# Decision 0003 — Progressive decentralization as an architectural constraint

- **Status:** Accepted
- **Date:** 2026-07-24
- **Owners:** Founding steward
- **Affected frozen components:** Product Constitution, Architecture Foundation, Frozen Foundations register
- **Related issues or pull requests:** Added by the progressive-decentralization mandate pull request

## Context

Calypso’s Promise originated from work on HealthDAO, collaboration with CureDAO, and lessons drawn from ConstitutionDAO-era collective action.

The long-term objective has always extended beyond a consumer application. The intended end state is a self-sustaining public-benefit institution in which users, contributors, maintainers, specialists, and other legitimate stakeholders progressively gain stewardship and ownership.

Earlier project efforts also demonstrated that decentralization can fail when authority is distributed before product utility, technical infrastructure, executive capacity, safety controls, incentives, and decision processes are mature.

The repository currently protects personal utility, sovereignty, consent, and open-source transparency. Its governance baseline, however, only states that no person should remain an irreplaceable dependency. DAO, token, and blockchain mechanisms are deferred without distinguishing deferred mechanisms from the non-deferred institutional destination.

Without a durable decision, near-term architecture could accidentally optimize for permanent founder control, permanent corporate control, non-portable infrastructure, governance theater, or a speculative token launch disconnected from useful operation.

## Decision

Calypso’s Promise adopts progressive decentralization and founder independence as permanent institutional and architectural constraints.

The following statements are binding:

1. The consumer application is the first operating surface, not the final institutional form.
2. Personal utility, safety, consent, and accountable execution precede research, markets, broad governance, or ownership expansion.
3. Early governance may remain maintainer-led and founder-directed when authority is transparent, constitutionally bounded, reviewable, and actively reducing key-person dependency.
4. Authority transfers through published evidence gates rather than dates, fundraising totals, user counts, token issuance, or ideological preference.
5. Each transfer must define scope, constituency, qualification, selection, term, removal, conflicts, transparency, appeal, emergency behavior, and rollback.
6. Governance must distinguish constitutional, safety, strategic, economic, operational, technical, and community decisions.
7. A future weighted model must account for affectedness, contribution, competence, trust, delegation, privacy, and anti-capture protections.
8. Capital must not purchase unlimited control, player rights, private-data access, or immunity from oversight.
9. Core participation and governance expression must not require broader health-data consent or unnecessary intimate disclosure.
10. Token, blockchain, NFT, and on-chain DAO mechanisms remain optional and deferred. They may be adopted only when they solve a validated problem better than simpler mechanisms.
11. Technical and organizational architecture must favor portability, auditable rules, replaceable operators and vendors, distributed recovery, documented authority, and durable institutional memory.
12. Founder independence is a success condition. The institution must eventually operate, govern, recover, and preserve its mission without founder intervention.
13. The project must maintain a public institutional roadmap showing what powers and responsibilities unlock at each gate.
14. The hundred-year objective is a design discipline requiring succession, amendment, treasury, archive, migration, dispute, anti-capture, fork, merger, sunset, and dissolution planning.

## Consequences

### Benefits

- Near-term centralized execution remains possible without confusing it for the permanent end state.
- Contributors and users can evaluate where the institution is going and what evidence unlocks authority.
- Product, data, infrastructure, legal, economic, and governance choices can be tested against founder independence.
- DAO or token mechanisms cannot become substitutes for utility, safety, or legitimate governance.
- Key-person risk becomes an explicit roadmap and architecture concern.
- The open-source project gains a credible institutional narrative capable of attracting long-horizon contributors.

### Costs and tradeoffs

- Governance and succession become first-class workstreams rather than future administrative details.
- Some expedient vendor, treasury, identity, or legal structures may be rejected because they create permanent lock-in.
- Authority transfers require evidence, documentation, simulation, and operational redundancy.
- The institution may decentralize more slowly than communities motivated by rapid token or voting launches.
- Maintaining transparent gates and institutional memory creates continuing operational work.

### Risks

- Progressive decentralization may be used rhetorically while control never transfers.
- Founder-reserved powers may lack measurable sunsets.
- Contribution weighting may create a maintainer oligarchy.
- Expertise weighting may create specialist capture.
- Affected-party representation may be difficult without compromising privacy.
- Capital limits may reduce some funding options.
- Governance complexity may exclude ordinary participants.
- Distributed authority may slow urgent decisions or obscure accountability.

These risks require public measures, bounded experiments, concentration reporting, appeals, removal procedures, and explicit gate reviews.

## Alternatives considered

### Permanent founder or corporate control

Rejected because it conflicts with the long-horizon public-benefit objective, creates key-person and mission-capture risk, and makes collective ownership performative.

### Immediate full DAO governance

Rejected because the current product, community, safeguards, identity model, economics, and operational capacity are not mature enough for responsible broad governance.

### Token-first decentralization

Rejected because financial participation does not prove affectedness, competence, contribution, or stewardship and may create speculation and plutocratic capture before utility exists.

### One-person-one-vote for all decisions

Rejected as a universal mechanism because decision classes differ in affectedness, expertise, duty of care, reversibility, and risk. It may remain appropriate for selected decisions.

### Contribution-only governance

Rejected as a universal mechanism because contributors are not the only affected constituency and activity volume can become oligarchic or gameable.

### Defer all governance design until after product-market fit

Rejected because architecture, legal, vendor, treasury, data, and brand decisions made now can foreclose credible future transfer.

## Validation or review required

Before any material governance transfer:

- publish the authority being transferred and its current holder
- define measurable entry and rollback gates
- perform capture and failure-mode analysis
- obtain specialist review appropriate to the decision class
- test the mechanism through simulation, tabletop exercise, or bounded pilot
- document minority protections, appeals, removals, and emergency powers
- verify that no player right or safety control is weakened
- report concentration of authority, delegation, and financial influence
- provide an understandable participant-facing explanation

Before founder independence is declared:

- complete a founder-absence exercise
- verify critical credentials, releases, treasury, legal, infrastructure, brand, and archives have legitimate continuity
- complete at least one real leadership transition
- verify the constitution can be preserved and amended without founder permission
- verify the institution can remove or override a founder acting against the constitution

## Migration and rollback

This decision does not immediately transfer authority or adopt a governance technology.

Migration begins by:

1. publishing `VISION.md`, `ROADMAP.md`, and the expanded `GOVERNANCE.md`
2. adding the mandate to the Frozen Foundations register
3. recording key-person dependencies
4. defining governance gates alongside relevant product and organizational milestones
5. recovering historical governance notes
6. piloting bounded, low-risk delegated decisions before constitutional authority

A specific governance mechanism may be rolled back when it produces capture, exclusion, unsafe delay, corruption, low comprehension, or constitutional conflict.

Rollback of a mechanism does not roll back the institutional constraint. A replacement must continue progressing toward accountable, founder-independent stewardship.

## Freeze impact

This decision establishes a new frozen institutional constraint and clarifies the existing product and architecture foundations.

It does not alter:

- the player promise
- personal utility before secondary use
- private-by-default operation
- meaningful refusal
- prohibition on rewarding broader consent
- open code and private production health data
- AI and deterministic-domain boundaries
- the deferral of token, blockchain, NFT, or on-chain DAO implementation

Material removal or reversal of progressive decentralization or founder independence requires a new constitutional decision record.
